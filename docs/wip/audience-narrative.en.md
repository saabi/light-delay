# Light Delay

*Audience narrative. Revision 16.*

## Prologue — Lights of Earth

Forty-three minutes and eighteen seconds.

That is how old Earth is when it reaches Jupiter. Every shout, speech, and raised sign on the screen has already become history before anyone at Proxima Station can watch it begin.

Tonight, one square holds two crowds.

The first points to the ancient throat beside Jupiter. For thousands of years its mouth has repeated the same visible-light sequence: a primer, an instruction, an invitation, and the timing of an aperture that may not open again for millennia. The Velari have never crossed into the Solar System. To the supporters, that patience is evidence of restraint.

The second crowd sees the same facts and reaches the opposite conclusion. Someone built a door inside humanity's home before humanity knew there was anyone outside it. The door speaks, but it does not listen. Nobody can ask whether its instructions are complete, truthful, or safe.

Rylen Harlan watches the hopeful half of the square for a long time.

He does not claim that the Velari could have entered whenever they wished. He does not know that. What he says is narrower, and harder to dismiss.

## Chapter 1. Embarkation

The mouth was found months ago. Its exact timetable was not. The full-aperture schedule arrives roughly thirty-six hours before departure: long enough to prepare a ship already at Jupiter, nowhere near long enough to bring a diplomatic team or replacement crew from Earth.

The mission therefore goes to the ship and people already close enough. *Celestial Ardor*: six thousand eight hundred tonnes, ninety metres by eighteen, built for research rather than ceremony. Six crew members. No diplomats. A calendar none of them can negotiate.

Loading is hurried, and hurry is loud. Pallets arrive out of order. Certifications are signed in corridors. In front of the manifest officers, Voss calls his second-in-command Commander, making visible what everyone already knows: hazardous stores, cargo movement, and final preparation answer to Harlan.

Below, Zao and Elin Rao run the post-loading check in the shorthand of people who have worked together long enough to make fragments precise. Zao reaches the end of the list and starts again from the other direction. Elin has seen this too often to comment.

Voss asks for departure status. Elin says they are ready. Zao asks for one more minute.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:a3-voss-on-time -->
> **Elias Voss:** "If Zao says we're on time, we're on time."

Zao signs only after the second pass. Elin leaves for her station. Zao remains to close the audit.

## Chapter 2. Transit

The *Ardor* releases, rotates prograde, and lights her fusion torch. The flight from Sun–Jupiter L1 to L2 lasts fifty-seven hours and forty-eight minutes: twenty-nine hours falling toward Jupiter under one gravity of thrust, then twenty-nine hours standing on the brakes.

It is a long time for six people to inhabit a small amount of metal. They eat. They argue about nothing. They sleep badly.

At periapsis, thrust cuts. Weight disappears while the ship turns from prograde to retrograde, and Jupiter crosses the deep ports with its rings lit from behind. Then the torch relights. Down returns, pointed the other way.

Sorell says what none of them has said aloud.

## Chapter 3. The Discrepancy

Near the mouth, thrust cuts for a second time. Precision requires coasting; coasting means weightlessness. Hair lifts. Pens drift.

Zao's audit, quietly extended beyond the point when anyone asked her to stop, flags a discrepancy in propellant.

It is small and exact. Across the accumulated burn, the *Ardor* has consumed measurably more fuel than a one-gravity profile requires. Earlier, the difference would have vanished inside metering tolerance and loading uncertainty. Only the completed leg makes it visible.

The ship has held her schedule perfectly. She has simply spent more to do it.

Zao checks again, because she always checks again, and because Harlan's face before the protest feed has occupied a corner of her mind since Proxima.

There are three ways to lose propellant that was never meant to be lost: a leak, an inefficient burn at the wrong mixture ratio, or mass the ship does not know she is carrying.

The second possibility leaves a signature. Starve a deuterium–helium-3 torch of helium-3 and the plasma becomes deuterium-rich. Side reactions produce more fast neutrons; the engine consumes extra fuel to maintain commanded thrust.

Near the outer reactor service bay, Zao's engineering dosimeter detects a weak but genuine neutron excess above the reactor's low-power baseline. The particles have crossed shielding, and a suit instrument is not a spectrometer. An off-ratio burn and a compact shielded source can look identical from here.

No fuel alarm is active. Zao has never treated the absence of an alarm as proof of safety. She follows the count aft, away from accessible fuel panels and toward the inner shielding vault.

## Chapter 4. Behind the Door

Weightless and sixty-five metres aft of the bridge, Zao meets Harlan leaving the inner vault.

He appears to be finishing an assigned inspection. He has not quite secured the door behind him.

His look lasts a fraction too long. He offers no explanation, and she does not accuse a man on the evidence of an expression. They pass without a word.

He now knows exactly where she is and what she may find.

Inside the vault, Zao finds a machine that does not belong aboard the *Ardor*. Its casing carries Proxima hazardous-store markings. It is a geophysical impulse package weighing between one and one and a half tonnes, designed to be buried beneath the crust of a sterile moon and detonated as a controlled seismic source.

At close range, the dosimeter count climbs sharply. The casing supplies the context her suit could not: the weak neutrons come from a fission primary.

Functionally, the package is a multi-megaton thermonuclear weapon. It has its own power, clock, firing controller, and tamper protection. It accepts no remote command, by design.

A local display is counting down.

Zao sets the number beside the flight plan and reads it twice. Zero coincides with the scheduled first-contact time and coordinates, moments after the far-side deceleration ends.

Three facts settle together. A thermonuclear weapon is aboard. It is timed for the encounter. And no ordinary exchange with Earth or Proxima can return an answer soon enough to matter.

Zao withdraws into the outer bay, where the local controller for the long-distance optical communications array is bolted to the wall.

## Chapter 5. The Warning

Harlan takes the service shaft forward. In microgravity, its segmented ladder forms the fastest route to the bridge. He intends to log his inspection complete and return to his station before the crossing.

The upper hatch opens immediately behind the bridge stations. The crew face their displays along the hull's curved wall, backs to the shaft. Harlan reaches the opening without being seen and pauses there, still silent.

Zao's voice arrives over the internal wireless.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:b2-zao-sabotage -->
> **Zao:** "I have found a grave attempt to sabotage the mission—"

Harlan abandons the report he meant to give. His wrist device activates a prepared local jammer, and Zao's voice is torn into static before she can continue. He disappears into the shaft before anyone turns.

Just below the bridge hatch, wired communications and camera trunks cross a physical distribution tray. Harlan unplugs them by hand. No credentials. No shutdown command. No useful log. Zao has lost her hardline, and the ship has lost its eyes toward the aft decks.

Then he descends.

He does not know whom Voss will send. He knows someone will come immediately. Whoever reaches Zao after him can be blamed, or at least made doubtful enough to protect the countdown.

On the bridge, Voss has half a sentence and a wall of static. He puts Elin on the outage and keeps Okoye forward because a sabotage report may mean the command deck itself needs protection.

Sorell's specialty cannot repair a communications failure. She volunteers to find Zao. Voss tells her to take Harlan as a precaution. She cannot find him, so she goes alone.

She uses one of the helical staircases in the central shaft, pulling herself hand over hand along rails and landings. Harlan is already below her in the narrower, straighter service route. Their paths never meet. With cameras and position records gone, neither leaves a reliable trace.

In the outer bay, Zao tries wireless, hardline, and emergency internal communications. The order of failure matters: interference first, physical disconnection second. This is not a fault. Someone aboard is silencing her in real time.

The long-distance optical array remains alive. Its aft emitter and local controller do not depend on the trunks Harlan cut.

Zao eliminates every obvious destination aloud. Jupiter blocks Proxima. A warning to Earth arrives after the detonation it describes. The pilot throat broadcasts but has never accepted a human transmission. Zao cannot improvise a message to the Velari in Sorell's untested grammar, and humanity has no confirmed address for their station. Even a beam somehow sent through the full aperture ahead of the ship would emerge in front of the *Ardor* and continue at light speed. It would wait for no receiver.

Then she stops speaking.

She overlays the flight plan with a path through ordinary space. The crossing is minutes away. The ship is about to take a shortcut her light cannot take.

Zao starts a new calculation and says none of it aloud. When the solution resolves, she releases a small breath.

Twenty-three hours, fifteen minutes.

The number does not promise help for her. It promises that the warning can outlive her.

With the main trunks dead, she records directly into the array's volatile burst buffer: the package, approximate mass and origin, rating, vault, countdown, neutron signature, and every clue that brought her aft. She signs the packet with her personal key.

No destination is readable on the console. Beyond the bay, her words vanish beneath hull ribs and the distant vibration of a reactor at service power.

The array slews toward an empty patch of sky and fires one compressed burst. Its buffer clears after transmission. The controller retains only time, energy, array angles, and ship attitude. It names no recipient and keeps no copy.

Nobody erases the message. There is simply nothing left aboard to erase.

Harlan reaches the doorway almost on top of the burst. He sees that she transmitted. He does not see where or what.

## Chapter 6. Murder

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:c1-harlan-recipient -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "Who did you send that to?"

Zao does not answer.

Harlan scans the emission summary. Confirming a destination means converting array angles and ship attitude into an inertial vector, then checking that vector against ephemerides. Someone is approaching through the ship. He decides he does not have the minutes.

Instead, he reasons from the same immediate facts Zao considered.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:c2-harlan-earth -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "Proxima is behind Jupiter. Earth will not know until we are already there."

Proxima is occluded. The throat does not listen. Zao cannot address the Velari, and their station has no confirmed communications path. Earth seems like the only place left.

He assumes her accusation is travelling home. It may condemn him in history, but it cannot stop the encounter-point detonation, and he expects to die with everyone else.

That prospect produces something more complicated than fear. If the attack succeeds, the accusation may also preserve his motive. He imagines a future in which people curse him first and later decide that he acted as their shield. He expects no rescue and no living gratitude—only a posthumous verdict.

His remaining work is smaller and uglier: kill Zao, secure the vault, leave before the approaching crew member arrives, and restore the cameras in time to record that person beside the body.

His mistake is not failing to read the telemetry. It is believing he already knows what it means.

Harlan sighs before closing the distance.

Braced against a service handhold, he attacks in microgravity and uses their combined momentum to drive Zao's head toward the rigid frame of a bulkhead.

The image goes black before impact.

There is a sound.

Zao's body remains in the accessible outer bay. Harlan closes the inner shielding vault and binds its isolated local lock to his wrist device. The door has never been on the ship's network. It now reports itself as inaccessible even to the captain and leaves no central access record.

Ahead of the ship, the throat opens. The *Ardor* crosses. The aperture closes behind her and returns to its patient one-way pilot state.

There is no abort now, and no way home before the return window. Of the five people still alive aboard, four do not yet know what the crossing cost. The fifth is climbing toward the bridge while his heart rate falls.

## Chapter 7. Custody

Sorell reaches the outer reactor service bay with no communications, cameras, or gravity. She catches Zao's drifting body and tries to help her.

She calls the bridge. Nothing works. She keeps trying because stopping would mean accepting what she is holding.

Sixty-five metres forward, Harlan reaches the service tray, reconnects the communications and camera trunks, and switches off the jammer. He rises through the bridge hatch already speaking: he found the cables disconnected and restored them.

The camera feed returns on Sorell holding Zao, transferred blood across her suit.

Voss asks whether Sorell found him. Harlan says no. He says he was aft on assigned duties, passed Zao entering the reactor bay while he headed forward, and never saw Sorell. The encounter with Zao is true. He volunteers it because a truth offered early is cheap cover for the only movement anyone may later reconstruct.

Voss sends Harlan and Okoye aft to determine what happened.

They find Sorell with Zao. Harlan turns the sight into a verdict before Okoye can speak.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:c7-harlan-accuse -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "How could you? Why?"

Sorell pleads her innocence while Okoye separates her from the body and takes her away under protective detention.

The *Ardor* has no brig. Sorell is restrained on the bridge, where she can be watched and questioned. It is provisional custody, not a finding of guilt, but nobody aboard can feel the difference. Harlan treats her as guilty. Voss fears she may be. Elin refuses to pretend she knows.

The evidence forms a coherent story. That is the danger.

The restored camera shows Sorell holding the corpse. She travelled alone during the precise interval with no communications, cameras, or reliable position records. The vault lock keeps no central history. Suit telemetry records condition, not location.

During questioning, Harlan adds a lie of exactly the right size.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:c9-harlan-lie -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "Before launch she asked me what it would take to delay the crossing. I thought it was professional caution."

Voss remembers Sorell under the protest feed saying *that isn't fear, it's respect*. He heard fear then. Harlan has handed that mistake back to him, sharpened into motive.

Custody is justified. Murder is not proved. Elin knows those are different sentences. What stays with her is how completely Sorell broke while holding Zao—and how quickly Harlan converted uncertainty into guilt.

Once clear of the far mouth, the ship settles into its far-side run and thrust restores weight to the decks: roughly seventeen point four million kilometres to the meeting point, about twenty-three and a half hours of acceleration and braking under one gravity.

Through the first half, Sorell remains under questioning instead of preparing the greeting. Voss quietly plans how to approach an alien station without his xenolinguist. Elin and Okoye work until exhaustion blurs the displays, then keep working.

Midway through the leg, thrust cuts and weight disappears for the third time. The crew secure themselves and their work as the *Ardor* rotates one hundred and eighty degrees from its acceleration attitude to its braking attitude. Hours of investigation become visible in a single maneuver.

The engine pushes toward the same deck again. One gravity returns and deceleration toward the Velari station begins.

## Chapter 8. Investigation

Voss orders a complete audit: the communications failure, Zao's final work, the mass reconciliation, every local instrument record that survived.

Restrained a few metres behind him, Sorell does not wait to be asked.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:d1-sorell-first-explanation -->
> **Lian Sorell:** "You're treating the first explanation as if it were the only one. That's exactly what I warned you not to do out there."

Elin starts with what Zao left behind, because Zao always left something behind.

The dosimeter logged a brief fast-neutron excess from the combined shielding-and-tankage zone. After passing through shielding and reaching a suit instrument, it proves exposure but identifies no source. The unfinished propulsion audit records extra fuel consumed, with the distinction among *lost*, *burned badly*, and *carried* left unresolved.

Harlan gives both facts one cause.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:d2-harlan-fuel -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "Lose helium-three and the mix goes deuterium-rich. More side-reaction neutrons, more fuel to hold thrust. Keep bleeding it and we miss the window."

Someone tampered with helium-3 feed or metering to force a mission-ending loss, he says. Zao interrupted the attempt, explaining why no alarm remains active. Any trained crew member can reach the emergency panels. A woman who wanted the crossing delayed has a motive.

The theory accounts for the fuel, radiation, interrupted warning, and Sorell's presence in one clean chain. Voss finds it persuasive. Elin records it as a hypothesis, which is not the same as believing it.

She and Okoye dismantle it as the records become available.

Tank inventory and pressure agree with engine metering. No helium-3 or deuterium left the ship unaccounted for. Commanded and measured mixture ratios remained normal. Plasma efficiency, exhaust velocity, and delivered thrust stayed inside their expected ranges.

The fuel was not lost. The engine burned it deliberately while guidance held the scheduled acceleration.

Measured thrust divided by measured acceleration leaves one explanation: the *Ardor* carried between one and one and a half tonnes more inertial mass than her manifest declared. No leak explains added mass.

Harlan adjusts. Perhaps Zao stopped the tampering before the mixture shifted for long. The revision keeps attempted sabotage possible, but no longer explains the fuel already burned or the neutrons. One convenient fact has split into two.

Elin and Okoye carry a portable radiation survey aft. The neutron count falls away from accessible fuel panels and strengthens toward the inner vault. It follows neither reactor power nor mixture telemetry. Something compact and stationary sits behind shielding.

The vault remains sealed by its isolated local lock. Voss's network authority cannot reach it. Every attempt without the correct wrist key fails.

They can support two conclusions and keep them separate. The *Ardor* carried unmanifested mass, which caused the excess burn. A compact neutron source waits behind a door they cannot open. The same object probably explains both, but steel prevents proof. They do not yet know it is a weapon or that it is counting down.

Elin returns to the records and finds another survivor.

During the blackout, the long-distance optical array fired a compressed burst from the outer-bay controller. Its payload is gone, but the summary retains the array angles and ship attitude. Elin converts them into an inertial vector.

It points to neither Earth nor Proxima, nor to any beacon or station in the catalogue. Zao aimed the strongest transmitter aboard at empty space.

Everyone heard the start of Zao's report before the static. Harlan can offer the obvious explanation without admitting that he saw the transmission himself.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:d5-harlan-missed -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "She was trying to finish the report she started—to Earth. The guidance trunk was down, so she entered the aim manually. She missed."

Elin does not look up.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:d5-elin-no-miss -->
> **Elin Rao:** "Zao didn't miss."

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:d5-harlan-frightened -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "She was frightened. She knew someone was coming."

The empty-space vector confirms Harlan's private assumption: a terrified engineer, a dead guidance trunk, a hurried manual shot, and an accusation flying toward nowhere—or toward Earth too late to matter.

Elin cannot disprove him. What bothers her is the premise his explanation requires: that Zao became careless. Harlan accepts that premise too easily.

Alone in his berth, Harlan sits with a result that cuts both ways. Zao's accusation appears lost, lifting a future condemnation from him. It also removes the only witness through whom Earth might understand what he believes he has done for it.

He would have continued regardless. The sentence matters because he has cast himself completely as the unthanked protector of a species.

Meanwhile, Elin tests the account nobody has tested. Harlan places himself aft during the blackout, and cameras he restored prove nothing about the time they were dark. His haste to convict Sorell could belong to an innocent officer, an accomplice discarding a partner, or a lone killer framing the first witness.

Elin does not decide which. She decides only that finding someone beside a body is not enough to close a case.

Ten to fifteen minutes before contact, with deceleration nearly complete, her console detects an incoming optical transmission lying exactly along the *Ardor*'s trajectory.

Surprise gives way to recognition: the array's packet format, then a signature header, then a name.

## Chapter 9. The Message

The burst arrives and decompresses. Nobody has played it.

Voss orders it opened on the bridge.

Zao's voice carries them backward twenty-three hours and sixty-five metres, into the outer reactor service bay. This time, the screen is not cropped and the recording is not silent.

The flight plan is overlaid with an ordinary-space light path. The *Ardor* will cross the throat minutes after the burst leaves and emerge more than twenty-three light-hours ahead of it. Zao cannot warn the ship where it is. She can warn the place where it will be.

Across roughly one hundred and sixty-eight astronomical units, the projected beam and the returning trajectory meet near the end of the far-side transit—with minutes remaining before the countdown reaches zero.

Twenty-three hours, fifteen minutes. The number was never a failure. It was the aim.

Over the restored image, Zao identifies the thermonuclear geophysical impulse package: one to one and a half tonnes, taken from Proxima's hazardous stores, sealed inside the inner shielding vault. Its fission primary explains the neutrons; its mass explains the extra propellant. Its multi-megaton charge is timed to the scheduled first-contact coordinates and hour.

The recording reaches the moment when a door opens behind her.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:e2-zao-goodbye -->
> **Zao:** "Just in case: goodbye. Harlan's at the door."

On the final words, the bridge returns to the present. Five people turn toward Harlan.

The message that seemed aimed at nothing was aimed at a moving receiver: the *Ardor*, after the throat placed the ship ahead of her own light.

Harlan says that voice and image can be fabricated. It is not a foolish objection. It is simply too late.

The message now joins everything Elin and Okoye uncovered: Harlan's unverifiable location, the speed of his accusation, the added mass and compact neutron source that broke his fuel theory, the burst he explained by calling Zao careless, and a vector that intersects the ship exactly where its flight plan always said it would be.

No single fact proves the whole story. Together, they leave no coherent alternative.

Elin verifies Zao's personal cryptographic signature and its timestamp, minutes before the crossing. The attribution appears on the main display.

Voss revokes Harlan's network authority. He orders Okoye to release Sorell, sends Elin aft to the vault, and tells Sorell to monitor the automated approach and finish the greeting.

Then he orders Okoye to remove Harlan's wrist device and restrain him.

## Chapter 10. Confrontation

Harlan appears to comply. He offers his wrist.

Okoye closes a hand around the device and glances at the display confirming revocation. He uses that fraction of a second to drive her into a console, enter the service shaft, and lock its bridge hatch from inside.

There is no immediate pursuit through the shaft and no way to know whether the lower access hatches remain usable.

Harlan knows where Elin is going. The package accepts no remote command, even from him. His remaining choices are physical: reach the vault, stop her, or trigger the weapon now.

Sorell stays on the bridge with an approach executing automatically and an unfinished greeting in her hands.

Elin has a head start. The speed-governed elevator carries her sixty-five metres aft. She reaches the inner vault and attacks the local lock.

The *Ardor* is still braking under one gravity. Harlan descends the service shaft on its segmented ladder, dropping between deck stops as fast as thrust allows and locking each access hatch behind him. The direct route is faster than the elevator's governed travel and far more dangerous than an ordinary passage. He reaches Elin before she solves the lock and attacks her.

Locked out of the bridge hatch, Voss and Okoye enter the central circulation shaft and take opposite helical staircases. Okoye detours at the first deck to test the adjacent service hatch. Locked from inside. One deck lower, Voss tests the next. Locked.

Two failures establish the pattern. They stop checking and run the spirals, but curved stairs, landings, and rails make them much slower than Harlan's direct descent.

Fifteen metres below them, Harlan has Elin against a bulkhead.

The *Ardor* completes her programmed deceleration.

Thrust cuts.

Weight vanishes for the third time. The navigation display continues counting down to the contact coordinates. The bomb is synchronized to that time and place, not to an engine command.

In microgravity, the staircases stop mattering.

Their railings and structural supports leave an aligned opening roughly two metres wide through the shaft's centre. Voss and Okoye arrest their spin at a landing and launch into it. They cannot fit side by side. Okoye goes first; Voss follows half a beat later. They cross fifteen metres through a ship that no longer has a down.

Okoye strikes Harlan, tethers him to a structural rail, and strips the wrist device from his arm. Voss pulls Elin clear.

Harlan is conscious, restrained, and out of moves. What comes out of him is not triumph.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:f4-harlan-answer-earth -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "You don't know what they are. You don't get to answer for Earth."

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:f4-harlan-time -->
> **Rylen Harlan:** "If they think we're harmless, they come closer. If they see a species that bites, maybe they wait. We needed time."

In two sentences, he gives them delay, deterrence, and his conviction that six people on a research ship had no authority to answer for a species.

Voss does not debate him. The clock is still running.

Elin opens the vault with Harlan's device. The package is counting toward zero.

Zao's warning has already given her what she has no time to discover: independent power and tamper protection. Diagnostics confirm redundant firing power. Cutting cables or attacking the controller is a wager she cannot make.

But the package was built for science, and science required an exact detonation time beneath a moon. Its maintenance interface must allow precise clock synchronization before deployment.

Elin supplies a valid mission-time reference—drastically rewound.

The display reads four, three, two, one, then jumps to a date absurdly far in the future.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:f5-elin-delayed -->
> **Elin Rao:** "It isn't disarmed. It just isn't going off here."

Someday specialists can tow it into deep space or open it safely. Today it is a heavy object in a locked room.

Voss reports the safe interval forward. Sorell finishes the greeting while the others begin the long climb home.

Her console unfolds the simultaneous spatial relationships of the throat's primer into a sequence a human can read, then reverses the process. Branching points of light gather into the grammar the mouth spent millennia demonstrating.

The greeting will not be a flash or narrow beam. It will be spoken through lights distributed across the *Ardor*'s hull, preserving both the visible medium and the relational form of the lesson.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:f7-sorell-primer-return -->
> **Lian Sorell:** "They wrote the primer. This is the first time they'll hear us read it back."

The signal is ready. Sorell waits for the order.

## Chapter 11. First Contact

Voss returns to the bridge and goes to Sorell before he looks at the station.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:g1-voss-sorry -->
> **Elias Voss:** "I'm sorry."

He means more than the restraints. He misread her caution, sent her aft alone, accepted the first complete explanation, and brought the mission to the edge of first contact without its only xenolinguist.

Then Voss takes command and gives the order.

Sorell transmits the greeting once, for the first time. Light moves across the hull of the *Celestial Ardor* and into the dark.

Ahead waits a structure four to five kilometres across, filling the region promised by the schedule.

Silence.

Then the station answers.

Slow, coordinated branches illuminate its immense surface. The small pattern Sorell showed during transit becomes recognizable as a child's diagram of this: a grammar native to living bodies, extended by technology to the scale of a city.

The Velari understand the human attempt because their throat supplied the primer. The reverse is harder. The human AI labours over a fluent answer in a language people have only imitated, losing parts of it as everyone watches.

Sorell does not celebrate. She waits until familiar structure emerges—one motif repeated from the primer clearly enough to trust.

Approach. Permission. Meeting.

She turns to the others.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:g2-sorell-someone -->
> **Lian Sorell:** "They're sending someone."

The station's answer ends. Branching lights withdraw across kilometres until the structure is dark and still. Because light is the medium, darkness is not absence. It is the other half of a sentence. Silence is still a choice.

A transparent, self-propelled sphere emerges from the station. At roughly five metres across, it is a bead beside the structure and enormous beside a human being. A layered shell, faint internal haze, and sparse embedded nodes suggest pressure, environment, and propulsion without explaining them.

Sorell suits up and leaves the airlock alone on a safety tether. Voss remains on the bridge. The sphere closes the distance slowly through the station's solar shadow and stops well short of crowding her.

The *Ardor*'s greeting lights are dark. Sorell dims her exterior lamp and waits in near-total darkness.

Inside the sphere floats one Velari in its own atmosphere.

It neither shrinks nor disguises its scale. Eight flexible limbs gather into a controlled conversational posture, leaving room on Sorell's side, while one or two remain partly extended so their true reach cannot be mistaken. Even gathered, the being spans two and a half to three metres.

There is no face. Several small sensory elements orient toward her, and the long, selectively translucent mantle that is both body and voice turns with them.

At first, faint inactive branches beneath the surface only make the body legible. Then those nearest Sorell converge, coordinate, and hold.

Attention comes before speech.

Selected regions of the mantle unfold into deliberate three-dimensional patterns, repeating elements of the human greeting and extending them beyond anything humanity sent.

The Velari's speech becomes the encounter's only real light. It catches Sorell's visor, particles in the sphere's atmosphere, the shell's refractive edge, and portions of the being itself without flattening any of them into glare.

Something entirely unfamiliar is visibly working to be understood—and to make the attempt beautiful.

Beauty proves nothing about benevolence. It cannot prevent misunderstanding, disagreement, or deceit. Everything Harlan feared about humanity's ignorance remains true. What fails is his conclusion that violence was the only safe answer.

The light continues for a long time.

Before the *Ardor* turns toward the return window, Voss prepares an official report for Earth. He sends it through ordinary space with the long-distance optical array as an independent record. It may arrive after the crew. It will continue even if the ship misses the window.

He includes the discovery, sabotage, murder, delayed warning, intervention, and contact. He states that Zao saved the crew and preserved the meeting. Her signed warning and personnel record accompany the report, and her photograph occupies part of the display.

Voss stops.

<!-- audience-dialogue-id: audience:dialogue:g3-voss-time -->
> **Elias Voss:** "You made it in time."

He does not choose which meaning he intends.

Then he sends the report. It travels into the same silence Zao trusted a day earlier, carrying her name toward a world that will hear this story late—and will hear it from her.
